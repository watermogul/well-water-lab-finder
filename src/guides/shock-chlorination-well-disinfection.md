"Shock chlorination" means putting a strong dose of chlorine into a well and the house plumbing once, letting it sit, then flushing it out. Colorado State University Extension calls it "a one-time treatment designed to kill bacteria in the well and water system" and "the preferred disinfection treatment for private well systems because it is simple, cheap, and effective for most situations." This page compares three published sets of instructions: Texas TCEQ, Colorado State Extension and the New York State Department of Health (NYS DOH). We read all three on **2026-11-02**.

**This is a summary, not a substitute.** If you do the job yourself, print one agency's full instructions and follow only those. Don't mix steps from different sources. All three sources say to hire a professional if you aren't comfortable doing it. NYS DOH recommends a water well contractor if you have a "point well" or a jet pump.

## When to shock chlorinate

- **After a flood.** TCEQ: don't use a flooded well until the floodwater has receded, you have disinfected the well and plumbing, and a lab report confirms "the disinfected water contained no bacteriological contaminants." EPA says to get help from "a well or pump contractor to clean and disinfect your well before turning on the pump." (New York flood steps: [our NY flood/drought guide](/guides/new-york-private-well-after-flood-or-drought/).)
- **After a positive bacteria test.** TCEQ says its steps apply "any time you suspect that your well has become contaminated by harmful bacteriological contaminants, not just after a flood." EPA says that if a test is over a health standard, you should "contact your public health department for specific steps to follow" and retest. (What results mean: [reading your results](/guides/reading-well-water-test-results-mcls/).)
- **After the well has been opened or serviced, and to control iron bacteria.** CSU suggests chlorinating "once a year or anytime it has been opened or serviced" to prevent iron-bacteria slime.

**What chlorine won't fix.** CSU warns that thick slime biofilms can "bind with chlorine and inactivate it", and that *E. coli* and *Salmonella* "may be protected from chlorination" inside them. CSU also says that sometimes "replacing the well cap, casing, and seal may be necessary", or the septic system needs repair. If bacteria come back after disinfection, the cause is still there.

## Safety first (all three sources)

- **Electricity and water.** NYS DOH: "EXTREME CAUTION is advised". Shut off power to the pump, check for broken wire insulation or missing wire nuts, and wear rubber-soled shoes or boots. TCEQ: "Use the appropriate precautions to avoid electrical shock."
- **Chlorine burns.** CSU: wear "protective clothes, gloves, and goggles" and work in a well-ventilated area. If chlorine gets on skin or in eyes, "stop immediately and wash thoroughly with clean water."
- **Well gases.** TCEQ: before opening a pressurized well's pressure-release valve, stand "in the open and breathing fresh air", because vented air "may contain hydrogen sulfide, methane, or other gases."
- **Have other water ready.** TCEQ: line up drinking, cooking and bathing water "for at least 12 to 24 hours." NYS DOH: use other water "until your well test results show the water is safe to drink."

## What chlorine to use

- **Regular unscented bleach.** NYS DOH: "regular, unscented household bleach". Not "splashless", scented or gel bleach. TCEQ: not scented or "odorless" bleach, nor chlorine-free bleach or hot-tub disinfectants. Use bleach with an NSF seal or the words "meets NSF Standard 60" on the label.
- **Calcium hypochlorite (pool chlorine)** is allowed by TCEQ and CSU. TCEQ says to use the granular or powdered form "not the large tablets", make sure it's fresh, and check for the NSF Standard 60 label. Colorado State says gas chlorine is "too dangerous for private use."

## How much: the three methods differ

| Source | How the dose is set | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| **TCEQ GI-432** | By well depth | Under 100 ft: 1 quart bleach (or 1/8 cup solid hypochlorite). 100-200 ft: 2 quarts (1/4 cup). 200-300 ft: 3 quarts (3/8 cup). Over 300 ft: 4 quarts (1 gallon) or more (1/2 cup or more). |
| **CSU Extension** | Calculated for a **200 ppm** solution from the water volume | Water in the well (gallons per foot of water depth × feet of water), plus 100 gallons for plumbing, pressure tank and water heater. Then per 100 gallons: 3 pints of 5.25% bleach, 2 pints of 8.25% bleach, 1 pint of 12-17% bleach, 11 oz of 25-30% powder, or 4 oz of 65-75% tablets. Check the label for % chlorine. |
| **NYS DOH** | Fixed amount | 2 quarts bleach in 10 gallons of water poured into the well during recirculation, then 2 more quarts in 10 gallons at the end (some of it used to rinse the well cap). |

CSU's gallons of water per foot of casing (Table 1): 2-inch 0.16, 3-inch 0.37, 4-inch 0.65, 5-inch 1.02, 6-inch 1.47, 8-inch 2.61, 10-inch 4.08, 12-inch 5.87. CSU's worked example: a 6-inch well, 150 ft deep, with water at 50 ft holds about 100 ft × 1.47 = 147 gallons. Add 100 gallons for plumbing, for about 250 gallons. That needs 3 pints of 5.25% bleach per 100 gallons. Bleach strength varies, so read the label.

## The steps, in outline

The three sources share the same basic sequence:

1. **Power off; open the well.** Turn off the pump. Remove the well cap, or with TCEQ's method the access plug. For a pressurized well, TCEQ says to first release the pressure at the pressure-release valve. CSU says to scrub the cap and casing first with a strong chlorine solution ("1/2 gallon of chlorine laundry bleach per 5 gallons water").
2. **Add the chlorine and recirculate.** Pour in the (diluted) chlorine. Then run a hose from the nearest faucet back down the well so the chlorinated water circulates and washes the casing. Recirculation time: NYS DOH says one hour; TCEQ says one hour (non-pressurized well); CSU says "at least 15 to 20 minutes". For a pressurized well, TCEQ has you replace the plug and let the well sit at least 12 hours instead.
3. **Pull chlorine through every tap.** Open each faucet (hot and cold, inside and outside, toilets, refrigerator water line) until you smell chlorine, then close it. CSU: first "disconnect or bypass any carbon filters or reverse osmosis equipment," and also run the washing machine and dishwasher.
4. **Let it stand.** NYS DOH: "at least 8 hours, preferably 12 to 24 hours," with the pump off. TCEQ: "at least overnight—if you can, let it remain for 24 hours." CSU: a 200 ppm solution "for a period of at least 2 hours is desired – preferably overnight." Don't drink, cook, bathe or wash with this water.
5. **Flush it out.** Run the water until the chlorine smell is gone. TCEQ: start with the outside faucet farthest from the well and do all outside faucets before any inside ones, "otherwise, you might flood the septic system." CSU: "Do not allow more than 100 gallons of chlorine-treated water to enter the septic system", and flush onto an area "where desirable vegetation will not be harmed... far away from any surface water or stream." NYS DOH: point the hose "away from grass and shrubbery."
6. **Water heater.** TCEQ and CSU both say to turn off the water heater during treatment. TCEQ also says to drain it and refill it. Follow the manufacturer's directions.

## Retest before you drink it

- TCEQ: "You shouldn't drink or cook with water from your well until a bacteriological lab confirms that the water is free of indicators of fecal contamination." TCEQ allows about two days for results.
- CSU: "it is advisable to retest the water for bacteria after one week". When retesting after disinfection, CSU says to sample "as close to the well as possible."
- Make sure the chlorine is gone before you sample. See [how to collect a well water sample](/guides/how-to-collect-a-well-water-sample/), and find a certified lab on our [state lab pages](/labs/).

## What we left out

- **Costs.** None of these sources gives a price for doing it yourself or for hiring a contractor, so we don't either.
- **Irrigation and livestock wells.** CSU has a separate procedure for these. It's not covered here.
- **Your state's rules.** CSU notes that in Colorado, disinfecting a well with chemicals other than chlorine requires a variance from the Colorado Division of Water Resources. Other states may have their own rules. Ask your local health department.
