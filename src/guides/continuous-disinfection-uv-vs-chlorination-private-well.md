"Continuous disinfection" means equipment that treats **all of the water entering the house, all the time**, not a one-time dose. For a household well that usually means an **ultraviolet (UV) light unit** or a **chlorinator**. This page compares the two using four university extension publications and Ohio's private-water-system rule. We read all five sources on **2026-11-26**.

**This page doesn't recommend a brand, a dealer or a price.** None of the sources prices equipment or installation, so we don't either. (Penn State's one cost figure is the electricity for a UV unit, below.)

## First: do you actually need it?

All the sources treat continuous disinfection as a later step, after the cause has been looked for and a one-time disinfection has failed.

1. **Confirm the result.** Penn State: before "making any costly decisions," make sure you used a certified lab and a sterile bottle, consider a second sample, ask for a count (not just present/absent), and follow a total coliform positive with an *E. coli* test. (How to read the report: [what your bacteria result means](/guides/what-your-well-water-bacteria-result-means/).)
2. **Fix the well.** Penn State: extend a buried casing above ground, slope the ground away, fit a tight sanitary well cap, check the septic system and move animal waste away if *E. coli* is present.
3. **Shock chlorinate and retest.** Penn State: retest "within 10 to 14 days and again several months later." If the follow-up tests are negative, "it is likely that a one-time contamination event occurred." CSU: retest "in one to two weeks." (Step-by-step: [shock chlorination](/guides/shock-chlorination-well-disinfection/).)
4. **Only if bacteria come back:** Penn State: "you will need to consider a continuous disinfection treatment system." CSU: if bacteria are still present, "the source of the contamination must be identified and eliminated before the water is safe to drink."

Nebraska Extension is the bluntest: continuous chlorination "should not be a substitute for a sanitary water supply," and "many water treatment professionals will suggest drilling a new well (or in some cases moving the source of contamination, such as a septic tank)" instead. It says continuous chlorination "should not be considered without consulting a professional and fully exploring other options."

Penn State's own numbers show why the order matters: a Penn State study found that about **15 percent** of wells with coliform bacteria could be fixed by shock chlorinating and installing a sanitary well cap, especially wells with fewer than 10 colonies per 100 mL.

## UV vs. chlorination at a glance

| | **UV light** | **Continuous chlorination** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it does** | Penn State: water is irradiated as it flows past a UV bulb inside a quartz glass sleeve. Adds no chemical. | A feed pump, injector or tablet unit adds chlorine (liquid sodium hypochlorite or dry calcium hypochlorite) to the water. |
| **Leftover disinfectant in the pipes** | **None.** Penn State: it "only kills bacteria inside the unit." CSU and MSU say the same: "no residual disinfection." | **Yes.** Penn State: aim for about **0.3 to 0.5 mg/L** of residual chlorine, which "is then available to kill bacteria that may enter the water after the chlorinator." |
| **Water it can't handle** | Penn State: "not recommended" where total coliform exceeds **1,000 colonies per 100 mL** or fecal coliform exceeds **100 per 100 mL**. Water must be "completely clear"; CSU: cloudy or turbid water decreases effectiveness. | Penn State: raw water "should be perfectly clear." Iron, hydrogen sulfide and organic matter use up chlorine ("chlorine demand"), so more must be added. |
| **Contact time** | None needed beyond flowing through the unit. | Penn State's general rule: about **30 minutes**. Standard pressure tanks are usually too small, so a holding tank or pipe coil may be needed. Nebraska: from pump to nearest faucet is "usually one minute or less." |
| **Pre-treatment** | Penn State: a sediment filter is often installed ahead of the unit; hard water may scale the sleeve, so cleaning or a softener may be needed. | Penn State: a sediment filter is routinely installed ahead of the chlorinator. |
| **Taste and by-products** | None from the unit. MSU: no disinfecting chemicals, "eliminating taste and odor issues." | Taste or odor some people dislike (Nebraska); a carbon filter can remove it. Chlorine plus organic matter can form trihalomethanes (THMs). Nebraska says THMs are "primarily a concern for surface water supplies" and exposure from chlorinated well water "is generally low." |
| **Upkeep** | Penn State: "minimal," but the bulb loses intensity and needs replacing "about once a year" (CSU: lamp life 9 to 12 months); the sleeve must be kept free of film. | Penn State: "significant maintenance": check the chlorinator, keep refilling chlorine. Nebraska: "requires continuous monitoring and knowledgeable management." |
| **Running cost the sources give** | Penn State: electricity, "perhaps $2 to $4 per month." | None given. Nebraska calls it "a costly and complex treatment process." |
| **Also helps with** | Nothing chemical. MSU: UV "will not remove heavy metals, petroleum products or pharmaceuticals." | Nebraska: oxidizes iron and manganese so a filter can remove them, and reduces hydrogen sulfide (rotten-egg) odor. |
| **Safety** | Needs electricity; no chemicals to store. | Penn State: liquid and solid chlorine "are poisonous and irritants." Nebraska and CSU: chlorine gas is too dangerous for household use. |

## Things to know about UV

- **Sensor and shutoff.** Penn State: some units have a UV intensity sensor that detects a weak bulb; it adds cost "but may pay for themselves in increased bulb life." MSU lists a UV sensor, "an automatic shutoff valve that activates if the system malfunctions" and a flow meter as features some units have. CSU says a unit "needs UV sensor to determine germicidal dose."
- **Storage tanks.** MSU: "If treated water is to be stored, then chemical disinfection using chlorine or iodine is also necessary."
- **Cysts (Giardia, Cryptosporidium): the sources disagree.** MSU says UV "can also destroy organisms that are resistant to chlorine, such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium." CSU's list says "viruses more difficult, cysts, and worms unaffected." Nebraska says chlorine at typical disinfection levels "will not adequately destroy protozoan cysts" and notes these are "not normally found in Nebraska groundwater." If cysts matter for your water (springs, surface-influenced wells), ask your health department; Ohio's rule (below) adds filters for those systems.
- **Point-of-use vs. whole house.** MSU says UV can be "single-tap or whole-house." Penn State's continuous-disinfection advice is for a system "that continuously treats all of the water entering the home."

## Things to know about chlorination

- **Where to add it.** Nebraska: in every type of chlorinator, chlorine "should be introduced into the water as close to the source as possible" for longer contact time. A tablet unit can drop pellets into the casing so water is disinfected before it is pumped.
- **Feed pump wiring.** Nebraska: a chemical feed pump "should be wired to the water well pump pressure switch" so it only runs with the well pump, plus a low-chlorine or pump-failure indicator.
- **Test free chlorine, not total.** Nebraska: "Be certain the kit used tests free chlorine, not total chlorine." It says the right free-chlorine level depends on your system, and household systems may run higher than the 0.3 to 0.5 ppm used by public systems because the contact time is so short.
- **Higher dose, shorter contact.** Penn State: contact time can be shortened by "superchlorination," which may need a carbon filter for taste. CSU gives two set-ups: 0.2 to 0.5 ppm for at least 30 minutes, or 3 to 5 ppm for about 5 minutes.

## Certification: no federal or state rule for most homes; Ohio is an exception

- **Nebraska:** "Federal, state, or local laws do not regulate continuous chlorination home systems; the industry is self-regulated." NSF and the Water Quality Association (WQA, Gold Seal) certify products against NSF standards; Nebraska says certification shouldn't be "the only criteria" but helps.
- **Ohio does regulate it** for private water systems (Ohio Administrative Code 3701-28-15, effective January 1, 2020). Key points:
  - **Who must have continuous disinfection:** ponds, springs, cisterns, wells with less than 25 feet of casing, and wells with 15 or more feet of casing that are properly built but whose aquifer is known or suspected to be contaminated with bacteria above the limit.
  - **UV must be NSF 55 Class A.** Units that "meet only NSF 55-2017 class B shall not be used for continuous disinfection." A UV unit used as the primary disinfection must have "an automatic shut-off device or warning device," pre-treatment for hardness, iron, manganese, TDS and turbidity as the maker or NSF 55 requires (the UV unit goes after any softener or iron filter), and an absolute filter of no more than five microns ahead of it.
  - **Chlorination:** the CT value (contact time × free chlorine in mg/L) must be four or greater, with at least 0.4 mg/L free chlorine in the piping after eight minutes.
  - **Shared wells:** if a UV-disinfected system serves more than one dwelling, either keep a chlorine residual of at least 0.2 mg/L in the lines or put a Class A UV unit in each dwelling.
  - **Sizing and labels:** equipment must meet the household's peak demand and provide at least 10 gallons per minute; a sampling faucet is required after each treatment step.

Other states and counties may have their own rules. Ask your local health department before you buy. (Ohio contacts and labs: [Ohio where-to-test guide](/guides/ohio-where-to-get-well-water-tested/).)

## After it's installed: keep testing

None of these sources says a disinfection unit replaces testing. Penn State recommends a total coliform test every year for every private well. Ohio's rule requires a sampling faucet after each treatment step, which is where treated water can be sampled. Use a certified lab: see our [state lab pages](/labs/), and [how to collect a sample](/guides/how-to-collect-a-well-water-sample/).

Remember what disinfection doesn't fix. Nebraska: chlorine "will not remove nitrate," heavy metals, hardness or fluoride. MSU: UV won't remove metals or petroleum products. (Other contaminants: [reading your results / MCLs](/guides/reading-well-water-test-results-mcls/).)

## What we left out or couldn't verify

- **Prices** of UV units, chlorinators, bulbs or installation. No source gives them.
- **NSF 55 dose numbers** (the mJ/cm² behind Class A and Class B). The Ohio rule names the classes but doesn't print doses, and we didn't read the NSF standard itself, so we don't quote them.
- **Ozone and iodine.** Penn State says ozonation is generally not recommended for disinfection (cost), and iodine is "no longer considered a permanent disinfection option"; we don't cover them further.
- **Your state's rules** outside Ohio. We only read Ohio's.
