Private wells are not covered by the rules that make public water systems test their water. The testing schedule is up to you. The agencies below give slightly different schedules, so we show each one next to its source rather than blending them.

## The baseline: bacteria, nitrate, pH, dissolved solids

| Source | What it recommends |
|---|---|
| EPA | Test your private well **annually** for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids and pH. Test more often if small children or elderly adults live in the house, or someone is pregnant or nursing. |
| Penn State AASL | Test **annually** for coliform bacteria and **every three years** for pH and total dissolved solids. |
| New Hampshire DES | Test for bacteria, nitrate and nitrite **every year**. Test the rest of its list **every 3–5 years**. |
| Southwest Nebraska Public Health Dept. | Test **every year** for nitrates and bacteria. |

## NHDES's fuller list for New Hampshire wells

NHDES recommends that well users and home buyers test for: arsenic; total coliform and E. coli; chloride; copper and lead (stagnant and flushed samples); fluoride; hardness; iron; manganese; nitrate; nitrite; pH; radon; sodium; uranium; and four PFAS compounds (PFHxS, PFNA, PFOA, PFOS). This is New Hampshire's list, so treat it as a model for what a state agency suggests, not a requirement for every well elsewhere.

## Test right away when something changes

EPA says to test your private well immediately if:

- there are known groundwater or drinking-water problems in your area;
- conditions near the well change significantly (flooding, land disturbance, new construction or industrial activity);
- you replace or repair any part of the well system;
- you notice a change in odor, color or taste.

## What the symptom or neighbor suggests

This table is EPA's chart of conditions and substances to consider testing for. EPA notes that not every substance listed is a health problem; some only affect appearance, taste and odor.

| Condition or nearby activity | EPA suggests testing for |
|---|---|
| Recurring gastro-intestinal illness | Coliform bacteria |
| Household plumbing or service lines that contain lead | pH, lead, copper |
| Radon in indoor air, or a radon-rich region | Radon |
| Corrosion of pipes or plumbing | Corrosion, pH, lead |
| Nearby intensive agriculture | Nitrate, nitrite, pesticides, coliform bacteria |
| Coal or other mining nearby | Metals, pH, corrosion |
| Gas drilling nearby | Chloride, sodium, barium, strontium |
| Dump, junkyard, landfill, factory, gas station or dry cleaner nearby | Volatile organic compounds, total dissolved solids, pH, sulfate, chloride, metals |
| Odor of gasoline or fuel oil, or buried fuel tanks nearby | Volatile organic compounds |
| Objectionable taste or smell | Hydrogen sulfide, corrosion, metals |
| Stained plumbing fixtures or laundry | Iron, copper, manganese |
| Salty taste, seawater or a heavily salted road nearby | Chloride, total dissolved solids, sodium |
| Scaly residues, soap won't lather | Hardness |
| Rapid wear of water-treatment equipment | pH, corrosion |
| Water softener needed to treat hardness | Manganese, iron |
| Water looks cloudy, frothy or colored | Color, detergents |

## Where to get the test done

EPA says to only use laboratories that are certified to do drinking-water testing, and points to your state's certified-laboratory program and your local health department, which may provide private-well testing for free. Start with our [state lab pages](/labs/) or EPA's [state-by-state directory](https://www.epa.gov/dwlabcert/contact-information-certification-programs-and-certified-laboratories-drinking-water).
