Many homes in Hawaii, especially on Hawai‘i Island, get their water from a roof and a tank instead of a well or a county line. The Department of Health (DOH) says plainly that **"rainwater catchment systems on individual homes are not regulated by the Department of Health."** No agency tests your tank or reviews your results, so testing is up to you. DOH does set out a four-part **screening test** it recommends, and it pays for most of one lead and copper test a year.

This page sums up what DOH and the University of Hawai‘i say about testing catchment water, and which labs on DOH's November 2026 lists hold each test method. We read every source on **2026-11-24**. Several of the documents are years old, and we say so where it matters. This is a summary, not medical or engineering advice. Questions go to the DOH Safe Drinking Water Branch (SDWB): O‘ahu (808) 586-4258; Hawai‘i Island 974-4000 ext. 64258; Maui 984-2400 ext. 64258; Kaua‘i 274-3141 ext. 64258; Moloka‘i and Lāna‘i 1-800-468-4644 ext. 64258 (numbers as printed on the DOH Rainwater Catchment page).

## DOH's four-part screening test

DOH recommends that catchment owners "perform and pass a screening test for E. coli bacteria, turbidity, lead and copper using a laboratory certified or approved by the DOH." To pass, the sample must meet these levels by these methods (table as printed on the DOH page, last updated July 23, 2026):

| Test | DOH acceptable level | Method DOH names |
|---|---|---|
| Turbidity (cloudiness) | 5 NTU or less | EPA 180.1 |
| E. coli | Absent | SM 9223 (Colisure or Colilert) |
| Lead | 0.010 mg/L or less | EPA 200.8 |
| Copper | 1.3 mg/L or less | EPA 200.8 |

NTU = nephelometric turbidity units. mg/L = milligrams per liter (= ppm).

DOH's own caveat: "the requirements here are less rigorous than what is required of regulated drinking water." Passing the screen is not a full safety test. It does not cover, for example, other metals, pesticides or the rat lungworm parasite (see below).

**Where to take the sample.** DOH says the sample "must be taken from a point after all treatment has been applied such as at your kitchen tap," and that "a qualified sampler will be required." DOH's 2014 FAQ explains: with no treatment, sample at any tap in the house; with whole-house (point-of-entry) treatment such as chlorine, UV, cartridge filters, reverse osmosis or carbon, sample downstream of the last step; if only one tap is treated (for example the kitchen sink), sample there.

**Who counts as a "qualified sampler"?** The DOH page doesn't define it. The 2014 FAQ ties sampling rules to VA home loans: under VA Circular 26-14-4, the lender ordered the test and sampling "may not be delegated to any party of interest to the transaction." DOH says that circular "is rescinded January 1, 2020." If you need a test for a sale or a loan, ask the lender and the lab who must take the sample. For your own peace of mind, ask the lab whether it sends a sampler or gives instructions for you to collect it.

**Other answers from the 2014 FAQ** (written for VA loans; DOH has not posted a newer FAQ):

- SDWB "does not provide any review of water quality results with respect to individual rainwater catchment systems."
- If you pass three of the four tests, you may resample for the one that failed. DOH strongly recommends fixing the system's design or maintenance first.
- A sample that is total coliform positive but E. coli negative ("absent") **passes** the screen.
- Treatment devices are a suggestion, not a requirement.

## The $25 subsidized lead and copper test

From the DOH Rainwater Catchment page (read 2026-11-24):

- DOH's Safe Drinking Water Branch "currently subsidizes the testing for lead and copper in individual homes served by rainwater catchment systems."
- You can use it **once per year**. It is for the owner or user of a **"legal dwelling."**
- **You pay shipping plus $25.00** for the analyses. DOH is billed for the rest.
- "AECOS Laboratory, Inc. is currently the only lab approved by DOH, and is participating in this program." DOH says to ask the lab if it participates, and that it **cannot pay for any other contaminants** or for work at any other lab.

AECOS Laboratory: 45-939 Kamehameha Hwy., Suite 104, Kāne‘ohe, HI 96744, phone (808) 234-7770 (from AECOS's own page and DOH's microbiology list). AECOS's page doesn't mention the subsidy or post prices, so call to confirm before you ship.

One thing we couldn't settle: AECOS is on DOH's **microbiology** list (for E. coli and total coliform), but it is **not** on DOH's November 2026 **chemistry** list, which is where lead and copper certifications appear. DOH still names AECOS as the approved lab for the subsidized test. If you need a lead and copper result that meets the screening test's "certified or approved" wording, ask AECOS or SDWB which lab will analyze the sample.

## When to test

DOH's page doesn't give a schedule. The UH CTAHR *Guidelines on Rainwater Catchment Systems for Hawai‘i* (revised 2020, "accurate as of October 2016"; CTAHR says its recommendations are the author's, not DOH's) suggests testing:

- when you move into a house with an existing tank;
- if you suspect contamination, or if the water's **turbidity, color or taste** changes for the worse;
- **periodically** if you don't regularly treat the water or if the tank can be contaminated;
- for **heavy metals** if the tank or home is older;
- after installing a new treatment system, especially if the pipes downstream weren't decontaminated at the same time.

CTAHR adds that once you're confident your treatment routine works, you can test less often "until something changes." It calls any test result "a snapshot in time."

**What the results point to (CTAHR):** a positive total coliform result means at least that "the system is dirty and could use cleaning and disinfection." Any fecal contamination means you should decontaminate the tank and the system. CTAHR says lead is "one of the most serious problems caused by metals in Hawai‘i's rainwater catchment systems," particularly in older plantation homes with lead paint, and also in newer homes with brass faucets. It also warns that acid rain, which in Hawai‘i is caused mostly by volcanic fumes, increases leaching. Note: CTAHR's book (accurate as of 2016) gives lead's action level as 0.015 mg/L. DOH's current screening test uses **0.010 mg/L**, so compare your result with DOH's number.

**Home test kits.** DOH's 2018 precautions sheet says kits "do not replace testing by a laboratory certified for testing specific drinking water contaminants," but can be a screening tool. UH Sea Grant posts information on PathoScreen presence/absence fecal-bacteria kits and notes they "do not test for other disease causing organisms (virus, protozoa, nematodes, etc.)." DOH says the CTAHR Hilo office number printed in the guidelines and on the CTAHR website for buying fecal test kits and pH/chlorine strips "is no longer valid." The 2018 DOH sheet's CTAHR kit link is also out of date.

**UH's agricultural lab is not a drinking-water lab.** CTAHR's Agricultural Diagnostic Service Center at UH-Mānoa offers water tests (pH, salinity, metals including lead and copper, nitrate). CTAHR itself says those tests "were designed for agricultural use, not drinking water testing," so use them only as a general guide. We did not check its current fees.

## Rat lungworm and other things the screen doesn't cover

CTAHR says rat lungworm larvae can emerge from slugs that drown in a tank and live in water for at least 21 days. It also says contact with contaminated water "could infect someone." Its advice is to keep slugs, snails and rats out of the tank and off the roof. The DOH screening test doesn't include rat lungworm, and none of the sources we read names a routine lab test for it. UH Sea Grant links to the UH Hilo Daniel K. Inouye College of Pharmacy for rat lungworm information.

## During volcanic activity

DOH's precautions sheet (rev. May 30, 2018, written during the Kīlauea eruption) says ash, laze and Pele's hair contain metals and are hazardous to ingest. Its advice: **temporarily disconnect the gutters** feeding the tank, and don't reconnect until the hazards have passed and the roof, gutters and tank have been washed clean. It also suggests a free-standing, non-porous canopy over mesh tank covers, and getting water from the County of Hawai‘i Department of Water Supply's emergency spigots. Check with the county for current spigot locations; the 2018 sheet doesn't list them.

The same sheet's routine advice ("prevent, protect, prove") includes:

- **Disinfection.** Chlorine, UV or both is "strongly recommended." As a starting point, DOH suggests **2½ tablespoons of unscented 5% household bleach per 1,000 gallons** in the tank, then mixing, measuring and repeating until free chlorine is about **0.5 mg/L**. CTAHR's guidelines warn that most bleach products are not labeled for use in catchment systems and that users do so "at their own risk."
- **UV systems.** Wipe mineral buildup off the bulb, check that it works, and replace it as the manufacturer recommends.
- **Materials.** Use non-metal plumbing and fixtures certified to ANSI/NSF Standard 61. Add only food-grade or NSF/ANSI 60-certified products to the water.
- **Maintenance.** Clean the roof, gutters and tank, trim overhanging plants, keep tank covers and vent screens intact, change sediment filters, and use first-flush diverters.

DOH's catchment page also says filtration and treatment devices certified by NSF, UL or WQA are "good for the contaminants that are listed on the certification. Do not assume that they remove contaminants that are not listed."

## What we could not verify

- **Which lab analyzes the subsidized lead and copper sample.** AECOS is not on the November 2026 chemistry list (see above). That's a question for AECOS or SDWB.
- **Who counts as a "qualified sampler"** now that the VA circular is rescinded. DOH doesn't define the term.
- **Prices.** The only price in any DOH source is the $25 subsidized lead and copper test. No lab on DOH's lists posts a catchment screening-test price that we found.
- **Whether county and DOH district labs accept private catchment samples.** Their listing is for public water systems.
- **A current source for test kits** in place of the defunct CTAHR Hilo number.
