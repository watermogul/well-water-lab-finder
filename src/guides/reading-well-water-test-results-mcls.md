Your lab report is a table of numbers. Each number means something only when you compare it with a limit. This guide explains those limits and the symbols labs use, and lists EPA's current numbers for the contaminants private-well owners test for most. We read every source below on **2026-11-01**.

**First, a key fact: these limits are not legally required for your well.** EPA says private well owners "are responsible for delivering safe drinking water to their households", and that private well water is "not regulated by the Federal Government under the Safe Drinking Water Act nor by most state governments and laws." EPA's limits are written for public water systems. Health agencies still tell well owners to use them as the yardstick. The Pennsylvania Department of Health uses the MCL as its "health screening level" whenever there is one, and EPA's Protect Your Home's Water page links to EPA's MCL list for well owners whose results aren't already compared with it.

This page is general information, not medical or treatment advice. If a result is over a health limit, EPA says to contact your public health department and have the well re-tested to confirm the result.

## The terms on your report

| Term | What it means (in the source's words where quoted) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **MCL** (Maximum Contaminant Level) | "The highest level of a contaminant that is allowed in drinking water." Set "as close to MCLGs as feasible using the best available treatment technology and taking cost into consideration." Enforceable for public water systems. | EPA NPDWR |
| **MCLG** (MCL Goal) | "The level of a contaminant in drinking water below which there is no known or expected risk to health." Not enforceable. Several common well contaminants (arsenic, lead, total coliform, uranium, PFOA, PFOS) have an MCLG of **zero**. For lead, EPA explains its zero goal is based on science showing "there is no safe level of exposure to lead." | EPA NPDWR |
| **TT** (Treatment Technique) | "A required process intended to reduce the level of a contaminant in drinking water." Used instead of a number for some contaminants, including lead and copper. | EPA NPDWR |
| **Action level** | PA DOH: if a contaminant is at this level or higher, "it triggers treatment or other requirements that a public water system must follow." Lead and copper have action levels, not MCLs. | PA DOH guide, EPA NPDWR |
| **Secondary MCL (SMCL)** | Non-mandatory guidelines for "aesthetic considerations, such as taste, color, and odor." EPA: these contaminants "are not considered to present a risk to human health at the SMCL." | EPA secondary standards |
| **Health screening level** | PA DOH's term: the level "below which health symptoms are not expected." It equals the MCL when one exists. For a few contaminants without an MCL, PA DOH gives its own level (marked "LP" in its table). | PA DOH guide |
| **Detection limit / "<"** | Penn State: labs "do not report zeros" because instruments only read down to a lowest level, the detection level; "when none of the pollutant is found in the water, the lab reports it as less than the detection level." A result like **&lt;0.003 mg/L** means none was found at or above 0.003. | Penn State, PA DOH guide |
| **">"** (on bacteria) | Penn State: "a very high level of bacteria was found, above the maximum amount that can be measured by the lab." Their example: ">201" means more than 201 per 100 mL. | Penn State |
| **J**, **U**, **RL**, **MDL** | PA DOH: **J** = an estimate between the reporting limit and the method detection limit; **U** = not detected; **RL** (reporting limit) is "the lowest reliable and accurate number"; **MDL** (method detection limit) is typically 2–5 times lower than the RL. | PA DOH guide |

## Units: mg/L, µg/L, ppm, ppb, ppt

- **mg/L = parts per million (ppm).** EPA's table uses mg/L unless it says otherwise.
- **µg/L (micrograms per liter) = parts per billion (ppb).** 1 µg/L = 0.001 mg/L (PA DOH).
- **ppt (parts per trillion), also written ng/L**, is used for PFAS (EPA PFAS page).
- **Bacteria** are reported per 100 mL: Penn State reports "MPN per 100 milliliters" (most probable number); PA DOH's table uses colonies per 100 mL. Some labs just report "present" or "absent".
- **pH** has no units.

Check units before comparing. Arsenic's MCL is 0.010 mg/L, which is the same as 10 µg/L or 10 ppb. A lab that reports arsenic as "8 µg/L" is reporting 0.008 mg/L, under the MCL.

## Health limits for common well-water tests

Numbers from EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations table (page last updated 2026-08-31), checked 2026-11-01. "Health effects" are EPA's wording for long-term exposure above the MCL unless noted.

| Contaminant | EPA limit | EPA goal (MCLG) | EPA's listed health effects (abridged) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Total coliform bacteria** | Public-system rule is based on the share of positive samples per month (TT/5.0%), which doesn't fit a single home sample | zero | "Not a health threat in itself"; used to indicate whether other harmful bacteria may be present | PA DOH and Penn State treat any coliform as a problem: health screening level "Absent (0 Col/100 mL)". |
| ***E. coli*** | (part of the coliform rule) | zero | EPA: *E. coli* indicates the water "may be contaminated with human or animal wastes" | PA DOH: "Absent (0 Col/100 mL)". Penn State: *E. coli* "indicate animal waste or human sewage is entering the water supply." |
| **Nitrate** (measured as nitrogen) | 10 mg/L | 10 | Infants under six months "could become seriously ill and, if untreated, may die" (blue-baby syndrome) | EPA's limit is for nitrate *measured as nitrogen*. Check how your report labels it and ask the lab if it's unclear. |
| **Nitrite** (measured as nitrogen) | 1 mg/L | 1 | Same infant risk as nitrate | |
| **Arsenic** | 0.010 mg/L (10 ppb) | 0 | Skin damage, circulatory problems, possible increased cancer risk | Some states set lower limits. New Hampshire's MCL for public systems is 5 ppb (see our [NH arsenic guide](/guides/new-hampshire-arsenic-radon-uranium-bedrock-wells/), checked 2026-10-28). |
| **Lead** | Treatment technique. Action level 0.015 mg/L (15 ppb) | zero | Children: delays in physical or mental development; adults: kidney problems, high blood pressure | EPA's table notes the action level for public systems drops to **0.010 mg/L** from the LCRI compliance date, **November 1, 2027**. EPA: "there is no safe level of exposure to lead." PA DOH also lists PA DEP's 0.005 mg/L standard for bottled, vended, retail and bulk-hauled water. |
| **Copper** | Treatment technique. Action level 1.3 mg/L | 1.3 | Short-term: stomach upset; long-term: liver or kidney damage | Secondary (taste/staining) level is 1.0 mg/L. |
| **Fluoride** | 4.0 mg/L | 4.0 | Bone disease; mottled teeth in children | Secondary level is 2.0 mg/L (tooth discoloration). |
| **Barium** | 2 mg/L | 2 | Increase in blood pressure | |
| **Selenium** | 0.05 mg/L | 0.05 | Hair or fingernail loss, numbness in fingers or toes, circulatory problems | |
| **Uranium** | 30 µg/L | zero | Increased cancer risk, kidney toxicity | |
| **Gross alpha** | 15 pCi/L | zero | Increased cancer risk | |
| **Radium 226 + 228** (combined) | 5 pCi/L | zero | Increased cancer risk | |
| **PFOA** | 4.0 ppt (0.0000040 mg/L) | zero | Cardiovascular, immune and liver effects; certain cancers | See the PFAS status note below. |
| **PFOS** | 4.0 ppt | zero | Cardiovascular, immune and liver effects; certain cancers | See the PFAS status note below. |
| **Turbidity** (cloudiness) | Treatment technique for filtered public systems | n/a | Higher turbidity is often associated with disease-causing microbes | PA DOH health screening level: 1 NTU. |

EPA's table has many more regulated chemicals, including solvents such as benzene and trichloroethylene (0.005 mg/L each) and pesticides such as atrazine (0.003 mg/L). If your report includes a VOC or pesticide panel, compare each line with the [full EPA table](https://www.epa.gov/ground-water-and-drinking-water/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations).

**No federal limit for radon in water.** Radon is not in EPA's drinking-water MCL table. Some states publish their own guidance. See our [New Hampshire guide](/guides/new-hampshire-arsenic-radon-uranium-bedrock-wells/) for NHDES's advisory levels.

### PFAS: where the rules stand (as of 2026-11-01)

EPA's April 2024 rule set MCLs for six PFAS: PFOA and PFOS at 4.0 ppt; PFHxS, PFNA and HFPO-DA ("GenX") at 10 ppt each; and a "Hazard Index" of 1 for mixtures of PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA and PFBS. EPA's PFAS page (last updated 2026-05-18) says that on **May 18, 2026** EPA proposed two rules for public comment. One "upholds the NPDWR for PFOA and PFOS" and would let water systems request two more years, to **2031**, to comply. The other would "rescind drinking water regulations for PFHxS, PFNA, HFPO-DA ... and the Hazard Index mixture." EPA's main regulations table, checked 2026-11-01, still lists all six. These are proposals, so the final numbers may change. We will update this section when EPA finalizes them. If your PFAS results are near any of these levels, ask your state health or environmental agency which levels it uses.

## Taste, odor and staining limits (secondary standards)

From EPA's Table of Secondary Standards (page last updated 2026-05-26). EPA says these are not enforced and are "not considered to present a risk to human health" at these levels. Several are common reasons a well owner calls a water-treatment company.

| Contaminant | Secondary MCL | Noticeable effects above it (EPA) |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminum | 0.05 to 0.2 mg/L | colored water |
| Chloride | 250 mg/L | salty taste |
| Color | 15 color units | visible tint |
| Copper | 1.0 mg/L | metallic taste; blue-green staining |
| Corrosivity | Non-corrosive | metallic taste; corroded pipes/fixtures staining |
| Fluoride | 2.0 mg/L | tooth discoloration |
| Foaming agents | 0.5 mg/L | frothy, cloudy; bitter taste; odor |
| Iron | 0.3 mg/L | rusty color; sediment; metallic taste; reddish or orange staining |
| Manganese | 0.05 mg/L | black to brown color; black staining; bitter metallic taste |
| Odor | 3 TON (threshold odor number) | "rotten-egg", musty or chemical smell |
| pH | 6.5–8.5 | low pH: bitter metallic taste; corrosion. High pH: slippery feel; soda taste; deposits |
| Silver | 0.1 mg/L | skin discoloration; graying of the white part of the eye |
| Sulfate | 250 mg/L | salty taste |
| Total dissolved solids (TDS) | 500 mg/L | hardness; deposits; colored water; staining; salty taste |
| Zinc | 5 mg/L | metallic taste |

Two cautions from PA DOH's guide:

- **Manganese also has a health level.** PA DOH lists a health screening level of **0.3 mg/L** for manganese (its own "LP" level), on top of the 0.05 mg/L secondary level. Above it, PA DOH lists possible nervous-system effects in adults, and says infants and young children "are at greater risk."
- **Sulfate** is "not likely to pose a health risk" but "may induce a laxative effect at levels greater than 500 mg/L upon initial consumption."

pH matters beyond taste. Penn State: water below pH 6.5 "may be more corrosive to metals in your plumbing system". That can raise lead and copper at the tap, which the first-draw sample in our [sampling guide](/guides/how-to-collect-a-well-water-sample/) is designed to catch.

## Things on your report with no standard

PA DOH lists several common results that have **no health standard** and are "not likely to pose a health risk" for most people. That changes if you're on a restricted diet: calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, iron and chloride all carry a note to consult your physician if you are on a restricted diet for that substance. PA DOH also lists:

- **Iron bacteria, slime bacteria and sulfur bacteria**: not a direct health risk. For iron and slime bacteria, PA DOH says they "may indicate the presence of disease-causing microorganisms".
- **Methane, ethane and propane** (often tested near gas drilling): not a direct health risk from drinking, but a suffocation or explosion hazard when the gas comes out of the water in a confined space. PA DOH lists a Pennsylvania action level of **7 mg/L** for methane and **28 mg/L** as the explosion and suffocation risk level.
- **Strontium**: PA DOH health screening level 4.2 mg/L (its own "LP" level).
- **Aluminum**: PA DOH health screening level 7.1 mg/L (its own "LP" level); secondary level 0.2 mg/L.

Penn State also notes that "some parameters have no drinking water standards at all." No standard doesn't automatically mean no risk. PA DOH: "Contaminants that do not have a health screening level may still impact health, but additional research is needed."

## A worked example (from Penn State's sample report)

Penn State's video walks through a sample report:

- **Total coliform 32 per 100 mL, *E. coli* 2 per 100 mL**: "Both of these results are above the drinking water standard of zero colonies, and both represent a health concern."
- **TDS 56 mg/L**: "well below the aesthetic drinking water standard of 500 milligrams per liter."
- **Arsenic &lt;0.003 mg/L**: none detected at the lab's detection level, so it's below EPA's 0.010 mg/L MCL.

## If something is over a limit

1. **Re-test to confirm.** EPA: if a result exceeds a health standard, "contact your public health department for specific steps to follow and have your well re-tested to confirm the contaminant's presence and concentrations." Use a [state-certified lab](/labs/).
2. **Bacteria:** EPA says high bacteria can sometimes be controlled by disinfection (chlorine, ozone, ultraviolet light). Retest after any disinfection; see our [sampling guide](/guides/how-to-collect-a-well-water-sample/).
3. **Infants and nitrate:** EPA's listed health effect for nitrate above 10 mg/L is serious illness in infants under six months. If you have an infant in the house, raise a high nitrate result with your health department and a health care provider.
4. **Treatment choice:** PA DOH says "a qualified water treatment company is the best source of personalized water treatment recommendations." Its guide also lists which treatment types (reverse osmosis, UV, softeners, aeration, nanofiltration) address which contaminants. PA DOH "does not endorse any specific water treatment system or brand," and we don't either.
5. **Health questions:** PA DOH: "Please contact a health care provider for medical advice."

Pennsylvania residents can also contact PA DOH's Division of Environmental Health Epidemiology at 717-787-3350 or dehe@pa.gov about results (as printed in the September 2024 guide, checked 2026-11-01).
