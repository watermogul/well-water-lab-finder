A lab result is only as good as the sample. A bacteria bottle touched on the inside, or a lead sample taken after the tap has run, can give a wrong answer. This guide puts the published sampling instructions of two well-water labs, two state agencies, two university extension services and EPA side by side. We read every source below on **2026-10-31**.

**The one rule every source agrees on: use the bottles your lab gives you and follow that lab's own instructions.** The labs below differ on details (how long to run the water, which faucet, how fast the sample must arrive). Where they differ, your lab's sheet wins. This page shows you what the steps are for and where to expect differences.

## Before you collect anything

- **Pick a certified lab and get its kit first.** EPA says to "only use laboratories that are certified to do drinking water testing", and that your local health department "may provide private well testing for free." Texas's TCEQ says: "You need a lab-provided sterile container and form." Find a lab on our [state lab pages](/labs/).
- **Check the lab's receiving days before you sample.** Colorado State Extension: labs "usually have specific times and days where they accept these water samples. It is critical that you contact the local health department, or private lab, prior to collecting the sample." Penn State's lab "cannot accept any water samples for bacteria on a Friday", and its kit must arrive "no later than Thursday of any given week." New Hampshire's state lab does not accept bacteria samples "after 12:00 PM on Fridays or the day before a holiday."
- **If you just chlorinated the well, wait.** New Hampshire's lab: "If you recently disinfected your water, make sure all chlorine is gone." NYS DOH says to test after the chlorine has been flushed ("the water should not smell of chlorine"), and if the smell persists "you may have to repeat flushing or wait several days before testing."
- **Decide: before or after treatment?** New Hampshire's form asks whether the sample was taken before or after treatment (softener, iron filter, radon or arsenic system). Texas A&M says not to take a bacteria sample from "any faucet that delivers softened, filtered or otherwise treated water." Colorado State suggests that if you have a holding tank or in-house treatment, "you may want to collect separate samples at the well and at the bathroom faucet." To learn what's in the well water itself, sample untreated water. To check that a treatment system works, sample after it, and tell the lab which you did.

## Why there are several bottles

A typical kit has different containers for different tests, and they are handled differently:

| Bottle | What it's for | Key handling rule | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sterile bacteria bottle** (Penn State: green label) | Total coliform and *E. coli* | Never rinse it. Don't touch the inside of the bottle or cap. Leave any white pill or powder in it. | Penn State, NH lab, NYS DOH, TCEQ |
| **Chemistry bottle** (Penn State: yellow label; NH: "Non-Sterile" and "Nitrate/Nitrite") | pH, nitrate, iron, hardness, metals and other chemistry | Penn State says rinse its yellow bottle three times with tap water, then fill to the top. Rinse only if your lab says to. | Penn State, NH lab |
| **First-draw lead/copper bottle** (Penn State: orange label) | Lead and copper picked up from your plumbing | Fill with the very first water out of the tap after the water has sat at least 6 hours. Do not run the tap first. | Penn State, NH lab |
| **Radon vial** (glass) | Radon in water | Fill to a dome ("crown") with no air bubbles. | NH lab |

The order matters. Both Penn State and New Hampshire's lab have you take the **first-draw lead sample first**, before any water has run, and only then flush the tap for the other bottles.

## Step 1: first-draw lead sample (only if your kit has one)

Penn State's explanation: "Lead accumulates in water from pipes as the water sits in the pipe." So the sample must be water that "has sat in the pipes for at least six hours."

1. Let the water sit unused for **at least 6 hours**. New Hampshire's lab says "overnight is best." Penn State says collect it "first thing in the morning."
2. Use the faucet you drink from most. Penn State: "usually the kitchen faucet."
3. Don't run the water first. Remove the lid, put the bottle under the faucet and catch the first water out. Penn State: fill it to the top and cap tightly.
4. Refrigerate it.

## Step 2: the bacteria sample (sterile bottle)

This is the sample that is easiest to spoil. Germs from your fingers, a dirty faucet or a swivel joint can make a clean well look contaminated.

1. **Choose the faucet.** This is where sources disagree most:
   - Penn State: an **inside faucet**, aerator removed.
   - New Hampshire lab: "a clean indoor faucet with cold water only. Avoid leaky faucets."
   - NYS DOH: the **kitchen cold-water tap** with the anti-splash screen removed.
   - Colorado State: a **bathroom faucet** with the aerator removed; "Kitchen faucets with swivel arms are not recommended." When retesting after a positive result or after disinfection, sample "as close to the well as possible."
   - Texas TCEQ: "Preferably an outside faucet that points downward and does not leak", taken "directly from the faucet, not through a hose", and "Do not take samples from kitchen or bathroom sinks."
   - Texas A&M: cold water only; not a hot tap, a hot/cold mixing faucet, a leaky faucet, a treated-water faucet, or a hose.

   Where all of them agree: **cold water, no aerator or screen, no hose, no leaks.** Use the faucet your lab's sheet names.
2. **Clean the faucet tip (if your lab says to).** New Hampshire's lab: wipe the rim with a swab dipped in **1 capful of bleach mixed with 1 cup of water**. NYS DOH: dip the tip in a capful of bleach, or flame it with a lighter or match for 10 seconds, after removing rubber seals. TCEQ: a butane torch for about five seconds (not on plastic faucets or plastic vacuum breakers), or spray with a 10% household bleach or 70% rubbing alcohol solution and let it sit several minutes. Penn State's instructions don't include this step.
3. **Wash your hands** (Colorado State, Texas A&M).
4. **Run the cold water to flush the pipes.** Times differ by lab: Penn State "about five minutes"; NYS DOH "4-5 minutes"; Texas A&M five minutes at full flow; Colorado State three minutes at full flow, then one more minute at a trickle; TCEQ three minutes at full flow (before disinfecting the faucet); New Hampshire's current form, **1 minute** after cleaning the rim. Follow your lab.
5. **Turn the flow down** to a gentle, steady stream. TCEQ and Texas A&M describe it as about pencil thickness (TCEQ: "1/4 inch").
6. **Open the bottle only now.** Hold the cap by its outside and don't set it down (Texas A&M). Don't touch the inside of the bottle or cap. NYS DOH: if you do, "STOP and get another bottle." Don't rinse it or dump out any powder or pill.
7. **Fill to the line.** Penn State and NYS DOH: to the **100 mL** line. TCEQ: "slightly above the fill line ... but not higher than the neck of the bottle." Don't let the water run over your hand into the bottle (Texas A&M).
8. **Cap it tightly right away** and label it with the date and time (TCEQ, Colorado State). Leaking samples can't be tested (TCEQ).
9. **Refrigerate it** until it goes to the lab.

## Step 3: chemistry bottles

Penn State: if you already ran the water five minutes for the bacteria sample, you don't need to run it again. Rinse the yellow-label bottle three times (fill it about a third, cap, shake, dump), then fill it to the top and cap tightly. New Hampshire's lab fills its "Nitrate/Nitrite" and "Non-Sterile" bottles from the same gentle stream right after the bacteria bottle. Refrigerate.

## Step 4: radon vial (if your test includes radon)

New Hampshire's lab: "Slowly fill the Radon vial to the top, forming a crown. Cap and turn upside down. If you see air bubbles, empty and refill." For a radon-only kit it says to remove the aerator and run the water for 5 minutes first.

## Step 5: keep it cold and get it there fast

| Source | Bacteria sample deadline | Other rules |
|---|---|---|
| Penn State AASL | Must reach the lab "within 30 hours of collection" | Ship by overnight mail; arrive no later than Thursday; no bacteria samples accepted Friday; pack bottles with the kit's ice bags |
| NH Public Health Laboratories | "Must be tested within 30 hours" | Radon and nitrate/nitrite within 48 hours; keep cold, "Do not freeze"; samples above 35°C (95°F) on arrival refused; mail by overnight delivery guaranteeing next-morning arrival, box labeled "PERISHABLE WATER SAMPLE" |
| Texas TCEQ (GI-433) | "Must be tested within 30 hours after you collect it" | Bag the bottle, keep it upright on ice in a small clean ice chest; ship overnight if you can't deliver it |
| Texas A&M AgriLife | "Six hours is best, but up to 30 hours" | "Many labs will not accept bacteria samples on Friday"; says mailing bacteria samples "is not recommended" |
| Colorado State Extension | "Deliver it to the lab within 24 hours" | Keep it cool |
| NYS DOH (pub. 6562) | No hour limit given | Refrigerate; keep it chilled on the way to the lab |

The safe reading: **sample early in the week, early in the day, keep it cold (not frozen), and deliver or overnight it the same day.** Your lab's deadline is the one that counts.

## If the lab says "unsuitable for analysis"

TCEQ lists this as one of three possible bacteria results. It can come from a sampling error, "if you rinsed out the container before you collected the sample or if the sample was cloudy or had too much color." Its advice: review the directions, ask the lab for guidance, and re-sample.

## How often to sample

EPA: test a private well **every year** for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids and pH, and test right away after flooding, land disturbance, well repairs, or a change in odor, color or taste. Colorado State recommends coliform testing "every one to two years." For what else to test, see our [what to test guide](/guides/what-to-test-well-water-for/).

## What we could not verify

- **Colorado's state lab sampling page** (cdphe.colorado.gov, "Collecting water samples") returned 403 to our fetch tools on 2026-10-31, so we did not use it.
- **One standard method for homeowners.** None of these sources is a national rule for private wells. Flush times, faucet choice and deadlines really do differ between labs, so we show the differences instead of picking one.
- **Penn State's printed kit sheet.** We used the lab's web page. The paper sheet in your kit may be newer; if they differ, follow the sheet.
