Ohio has no single state office that tests homeowners' wells. Ohio EPA says plainly that it "does not regulate or test drinking water from private wells." What the state does give you is two routes: **your local health district**, which regulates private wells and may run a homeowner testing program, and **labs certified by Ohio EPA** to test drinking water. This page walks through both. We read every source listed below on **2026-10-29**. Fees and services change, so treat every number here as "as of that date" and confirm by phone.

This is a summary, not medical or engineering advice.

## Step 1: call your local health district

Ohio EPA's private-well answer says: "Prior to calling a certified laboratory, you may want to contact your local health department. Your local health department regulates private water wells and they may have a program for homeowners who want to have their well water tested." The Ohio Department of Health (ODH) says the same thing: "Contact your local health district to learn what water samples they can collect."

Find yours with ODH's **[Find Local Health Departments](https://odh.ohio.gov/find-local-health-districts)** search, which covers every city and county health district in Ohio.

## Step 2: if your health district can't do the test, use an Ohio EPA–certified lab

ODH requires samples taken for new, altered or repaired wells to go to a lab certified by ODH or Ohio EPA. For everything else it says: "The department recommends that all water samples collected from private water systems for real estate transactions, or consumer information also be analyzed at a certified laboratory." Ohio EPA publishes the certified-lab list as a PDF. The **October 2026** edition is the current one.

The catch: Ohio EPA's list is for **public** water systems. It doesn't say which labs take samples from homeowners. Our [Ohio lab page](/labs/ohio/) lists every Ohio-address lab on it, but you have to call around.

## What to ask the lab (or health district) to test

- **ODH's recommendation:** "test a few parameters such as total coliform bacteria, E. coli, nitrates and arsenic on a regular basis to maintain a record of water quality." ODH notes that "Testing for all the contaminants for the federal safe drinking water standards is expensive and not necessary for most systems."
- **OSU Extension (AEX-315 and AEX-314):** test at least once a year. AEX-314 (2016) lists the annual minimum as total coliform bacteria, nitrate, pH and total dissolved solids (or conductivity). For stains or bad taste, it suggests iron, manganese and copper. For corrosive water it suggests a "first flush" sample for lead and copper.
- **The numbers ODH uses** (it adopts EPA's limits as "non-enforceable health-based" standards for private wells): nitrate **10 mg/L**; arsenic **0.01 mg/L (10 ppb)**.
- **New or repaired wells** are different. To approve a permit, ODH requires total coliform, E. coli and nitrate tests, and the health district collects those samples under rule 3701-28-04.
- **Suspect a specific source?** AEX-314 says to contact your local health department "if you suspect contamination from leaking fuel tanks, landfills or chemical spills," and suggests matching the test to the source (for example, a hydrocarbon scan near a leaking fuel tank).

For a wider list of what to test for, see [what to test well water for](/guides/what-to-test-well-water-for/).

## Sample day: rules worth copying

Ohio's private-water rule 3701-28-04 governs how health districts take **permit** samples. It doesn't bind a homeowner's own sample, but it's a good standard to copy:

- Collect at the system's point of discharge, **not** from hoses, outside spigots or yard hydrants.
- Run a well for **at least ten minutes** before sampling.
- If the well was just chlorinated, wait **at least 48 hours** after it has been flushed free of chlorine.

AEX-314 adds: use the bottle the lab provides, follow its handling directions (cold, dark, fast), and "check the lab schedule for sample acceptance. Some labs, for example, will not accept samples on Fridays or weekends." Wayne County's drop-off windows above show how strict the timing can be.
