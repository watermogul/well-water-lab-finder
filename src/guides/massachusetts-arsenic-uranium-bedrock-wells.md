If your Massachusetts home has a **drilled (bedrock) well**, arsenic and uranium are the two natural contaminants MassDEP singles out. Neither has a taste, smell or color; MassDEP says only a laboratory test can find arsenic. This page sums up what MassDEP and the Massachusetts Department of Public Health (MDPH) publish about them, plus radium and radon, and lists the labs that take homeowner samples and are MassDEP-certified for each test. We read every source on **2026-11-27**. Most of the state pages print no revision date, and MDPH's page says "Content last updated 2016", so treat the cost figures below as MassDEP's older estimates, not current quotes.

This is a summary, not medical or engineering advice. Health questions: MDPH Division of Environmental Toxicology, Hazard Assessment and Prevention, (617) 624-5757. Treatment questions: MassDEP's Drinking Water Program (MDPH gives MassDEP's number as 617-292-5500).

**Who's in charge:** MassDEP says it "does not regulate private drinking water wells." Under state law (MGL Ch. 111 s. 122), your **local board of health** regulates private wells and sets any private-well water standards, which MassDEP says are "typically the same" as the public drinking water standards. Check your town's private well regulation, especially when buying or building.

## How common is it? The USGS bedrock well study

Starting in 2008, the U.S. Geological Survey, with MassDEP funding, tested **478 private bedrock wells in 116 communities** in east-central and northeastern Massachusetts (report published 2011). MassDEP's summary of the results:

| | Arsenic | Uranium |
|---|---|---|
| Samples over the public drinking water standard | About **13%** (over 10 ppb) | **Less than 3%** (over 30 ppb) |
| USGS estimate for the ~90,000 residential bedrock wells in the study area | About **5,700** may exceed the standard; about **3,800** of those used without arsenic treatment | About **3,300** may exceed the standard; about **3,000** of those used without uranium treatment |
| What predicts it | Bedrock type, and especially **closeness to the Clinton-Newbury fault zone** (runs from near the eastern end of the Massachusetts-Connecticut border to the Merrimack River Valley) | Bedrock type; **granite and pegmatite** bedrock had the highest chance of exceeding |

Two cautions from MassDEP itself:

- The study area is where MassDEP believes **most high-arsenic areas** are, but the sampling "is not sufficient enough for us to know for certain" about other places.
- For uranium, MassDEP believes **"many areas located outside of the study area"** also have a higher chance of exceeding the uranium standard "and other radiological standards such as radon and radium", especially where the bedrock is mapped as granite or pegmatite.

MassDEP's arsenic FAQ adds that arsenic is found "usually in bedrock aquifers in the central part of the state and in the Merrimack River Valley", and that dug or shallow wells "may contain arsenic" too. Past activities that could leave arsenic behind include apple orchard spraying, coal ash disposal and some pressure-treated wood.

**Is your address in a higher-probability zone?** MassDEP points to its **[Well Locator Tool](https://maps.massgis.digital.mass.gov/images/dep/pbwell/pbwell_map.html)** (enter a street address and town). MassDEP also publishes statewide overview maps for [arsenic](https://www.mass.gov/doc/probability-of-exceeding-the-arsenic-drinking-water-standard-in-private-drinking-water-wells-in-massachusetts-map/download) and [uranium](https://www.mass.gov/doc/probability-of-exceeding-the-uranium-drinking-water-standard-in-private-drinking-water-wells-in-massachusetts-map/download). The town-by-town PDF maps "have been archived" and are being replaced by a GIS application that MassDEP says "will be online in late 2026"; until then MassDEP asks people to request a town map from program.director-dwp@mass.gov. A low-probability zone is not a clean result: MassDEP says "the only way to know for sure is to have your well tested."

## The numbers that matter

| Contaminant | Limit | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Arsenic | **10 ppb** (0.010 mg/L) | EPA and state public drinking water standard (EPA lowered it from 50 ppb in 2001, effective 2006). |
| Uranium | **30 ppb** (30 µg/L) | EPA public drinking water standard (2003). |
| Gross alpha | **15 pCi/L** | EPA standard; also MassDEP's screening trigger for uranium testing (below). |
| Radium-226 + radium-228 | **5 pCi/L** combined | EPA standard. |
| Radon in water | **10,000 pCi/L** | Massachusetts guideline (MassDEP Office of Research and Standards Guideline) "triggering indoor air testing". There is no EPA radon-in-water standard. |

The arsenic, uranium, gross alpha and radium values match EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations table (checked 2026-11-27). Note that MassDEP's own pages differ in how they describe radon: the radionuclides FAQ calls 10,000 pCi/L the "Current Massachusetts Regulatory Limit", while the Protect Your Family guide says there are "no federal or state standards for radon in drinking water, only suggested action levels." Either way, MassDEP says to check your **indoor air** radon if water radon is high.

### MassDEP's arsenic action points

From MassDEP's arsenic FAQ:

- **Above 10 ppb:** "get drinking water from another source or install a home treatment device." MassDEP says this matters most for children and during pregnancy.
- **Above 200 ppb:** "immediately stop drinking the water" until you have another source or treatment.
- **Showering and bathing** are safe unless arsenic is over **500 ppb**; arsenic "is not absorbed through the skin and does not evaporate into the air."

MassDEP's study FAQ adds that while you wait for results it is generally safe to drink and bathe ("the probability that anyone's well poses a health concern is very low"), and you may use bottled water in the meantime if concerned.

## What to test, and how often

MassDEP's **Protect Your Family** table recommends, for every private well, testing "initially and then at a minimum once every ten years, or as otherwise required by the local Board of Health" for:

- the **Standard Analysis**, which includes **arsenic** (plus chloride, copper, fluoride, hardness, iron, lead, manganese, pH and sodium);
- **radon**;
- a **gross alpha screen (bedrock wells only)**;
- VOCs and PFAS6.

(Bacteria and nitrate/nitrite: at least yearly.) MassDEP and MDPH "recommend that all homeowners test their wells for arsenic and radionuclides such as uranium."

### The radionuclide sequence MassDEP recommends

MassDEP's radionuclides FAQ says to ask the lab for **gross alpha first**, then:

1. Gross alpha **below 5 pCi/L**: no radium-226, radium-228 or uranium testing needed.
2. Gross alpha **5 pCi/L or more**: test for **radium-226 and radium-228**.
3. Gross alpha **15 pCi/L or more**: also test for **uranium**.

Because radionuclide levels "can vary substantially based on rainfall and other factors", MassDEP says to take **at least two samples, a month or two apart if possible**, before drawing conclusions. Radon in water is a separate test; see the radon note under "Labs" below.

### If arsenic is over 10 ppb: ask for arsenic speciation

Inorganic arsenic occurs as **arsenic 3** and **arsenic 5**. MassDEP says arsenic 3 is "much more difficult to remove" and must be oxidized to arsenic 5 first, so if total arsenic is over the standard, have a sample tested for **arsenic speciation** before choosing treatment. MassDEP notes:

- Some labs give you a **field filtration kit** to separate the arsenic 3 sample at home; others filter in the lab. MassDEP calls field filtration "preferable, especially if the water sample contains a significant concentration of iron."
- If the lab filters, ask which day(s) to collect and deliver so the sample gets filtered quickly.
- Speciation is "at extra cost" and may need extra bottles.

MassDEP's only list of which labs offer speciation is its **2018** spreadsheet (file last saved February 2018). It marked speciation "Yes" for Analytical Balance, Barnstable County, Granite State Analytical, Nashoba Analytical, Premier Laboratory (Dayville, CT, same address as today's Microbac Dayville) and Underwriters Laboratories (South Bend, IN, same address as today's Eurofins South Bend). Several of those names have since changed, so **call the lab and ask** before relying on it. MassDEP certification for speciation is not something LabCert lets you search for.

## Treatment basics from MassDEP

- **Kitchen tap is usually enough for arsenic and uranium.** MassDEP: in Massachusetts the concentrations are "typically only of concern for ingestion", so treating drinking and cooking water is "typically all that is required." Consider whole-house treatment if arsenic is over **500 ppb** ("extremely rare in Massachusetts").
- **Radon needs whole-house treatment.** Radon escapes from showers, faucets and appliances into indoor air, so MassDEP says point-of-entry treatment (aeration preferred) is required.
- **Arsenic methods:** anion exchange, reverse osmosis (RO), activated alumina and other adsorptive media. MassDEP warns that **RO "is not effective at removing arsenic 3"**, and activated alumina and most adsorptive media remove arsenic 3 poorly or not at all; oxidants such as chlorine bleach, hydrogen peroxide or ozone convert it to arsenic 5. Iron and manganese may need removing first. Adsorptive cartridges are replaced "about every six months" and can go out as non-hazardous waste.
- **Uranium:** reverse osmosis or anion exchange. **Radium:** cation exchange, manganese greensand filters or RO.
- **Where the reject water goes:** MassDEP says RO reject water from a kitchen-tap unit at a 1-4 unit home may typically go to a Title 5 septic system only if raw-water total arsenic is under 1 mg/L (1,000 ppb); for uranium limits, contact MassDEP. Whole-house treatment wastewater may not go to a Title 5 system; it needs a sewer connection or a separate dry well, and a dry well needs a MassDEP Underground Injection Control registration except at single-family homes.
- **Help with cost:** MassDEP points very-low-income homeowners in rural areas (generally towns up to 10,000 people, some up to 20,000) to **USDA Rural Development's Section 504 home repair loans and grants**, which it says can cover on-site water treatment. Eligibility is set by USDA; check [USDA Rural Development](https://www.rd.usda.gov/ma).

After installing anything, test the **treated** water to confirm it works.

## Health questions

MDPH (2016 content) says short-term exposure to high inorganic arsenic (300 ppb or more) can cause nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and other effects, and long-term exposure to lower levels can cause skin changes, nerve damage and higher risk of several cancers. For uranium, the main concern is **kidney damage** (EPA set the uranium standard mainly on chemical toxicity). MassDEP's radionuclides FAQ adds that radium can build up in bone. MDPH says a **urine test** is the best way to check recent exposure (a few days) and can help you or your doctor choose a test that separates inorganic arsenic from the less toxic organic arsenic in some foods. In the USGS study, MDPH urine-tested 37 participants; some had above-typical arsenic or uranium, but "none had arsenic or uranium in their urine at levels of health concern." MassDEP also recommends giving pets water that meets the human standards.
