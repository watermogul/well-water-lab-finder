Floods and droughts hit private wells in opposite ways. A flood can push germs and chemicals into a well. A drought can drop the water level until the pump draws air. New York's Department of Health (NYS DOH) and Department of Environmental Conservation (DEC) both publish advice for each case. This page sums it up, with quotes where the wording matters. We read every source listed below on **2026-10-30**.

This is a summary, not medical or engineering advice. Your **local health department** is the official contact for both problems, and NYS DOH's [contact map](https://www.health.ny.gov/EnvironmentalContacts) lists every county and district office.

## After a flood

NYS DOH's flood booklet ([publication 6562](https://health.ny.gov/publications/6562/index.htm), PDF printed 2/17) is the main state source. It starts: "After flooding, water from private wells may not be safe to drink." If you think your well was contaminated, "stop using your well water for drinking and cooking purposes." Until the well is restored, DOH says bottled water is best for drinking and preparing food. Ask your local health department about other safe water sources.

**Gasoline, heating oil or other chemicals?** Don't use the well. Call your local health department or the **DEC Spill Hotline at (800) 457-7362**. DOH warns: "Boiling or disinfecting water will not remove oil and other chemicals in drinking water."

DOH lists four steps to get a flooded well back into service: **assessment, repair and flushing, disinfection, sampling.**

### 1. Assess (power off first)

DOH's first step is "TURN OFF THE POWER TO THE WELL." Then check:

- **Was the well flooded?** If you weren't there to see it, look for debris, mud, or water or mud stains on the well.
- **The ground around the well.** Erosion or broken ground can let surface water run down into the well.
- **Electrical parts and wires.** Don't touch the wires. "If electrical connections or controls located outside the well casing remain submerged, do not turn on the pump." If anything electrical got wet, call an electrician or well professional.
- **The casing.** A bent or cracked casing can let water, sediment and debris in.
- **The cap and seal.** If sediment and debris got into the well, "call a professional before restarting the well."

Most pumps sit down inside the casing, so you probably can't inspect yours. DOH says not to turn the pump on until the well has been checked and repaired as needed. DEC has a [search tool](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/water/water-quantity/water-well-contractor-program) for **registered water well contractors**, and your local health department can also help you find one.

### 2. Repair and flush

DOH's four flushing steps are **clean** (remove mud and debris from the casing and cap), **re-grade** (slope the ground so water runs away from the casing), **start the pump** (after it has been inspected, repaired or replaced), and **flush**. To flush, pump water out through a hose from an outside faucet until it runs clear. Send the water to a drainage path, not into your septic system or sewer, because "after flooding, both septics and public sewers may be overwhelmed." DOH says this "may take thirty minutes, or it could take several hours or days."

### 3. Disinfect the well and plumbing

DOH's method circulates a strong bleach solution through the well and the house plumbing. In outline (follow the [full 12 steps](https://health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/well_disinfect.htm) and their electrical-safety warnings):

- Mix **2 quarts of regular, unscented household bleach in 10 gallons of water**. Don't use "splashless", scented or gel bleach.
- Pour it into the open casing while a hose from an outdoor faucet runs back into the well. Run every faucet until you smell chlorine. Then keep recirculating through the hose for **one hour**.
- Mix **2 more quarts of bleach in 10 gallons of water**. Use about half a gallon of it to rinse the well cap and seal. With the pump power off, pour the rest into the well and replace the cap.
- Let the well stand idle **at least 8 hours, preferably 12 to 24 hours**, with the pump power off.
- Flush the chlorinated water out through an outside hose, aimed away from grass and shrubs. Then run every faucet until the chlorine smell is gone.

DOH recommends hiring a water well contractor to disinfect the well if you have a **"point well"** or a **jet pump**.

### 4. Test for bacteria, and keep using other water until the result is back

After the chlorine is gone ("the water should not smell of chlorine"), test the water. If the chlorine smell won't go away, "you may have to repeat flushing or wait several days before testing." DOH's sampling steps:

1. Get a sterile "BacT" bottle from a **New York State certified (ELAP) lab** or your local health department.
2. Don't rinse the bottle, and don't touch the inside of the bottle or cap. If you do, get another bottle.
3. Remove the anti-splash screen from the kitchen cold tap. Disinfect the faucet tip by dipping it in a capful of bleach or "flaming" it for 10 seconds (take off any rubber seals first).
4. Run the cold water for **4–5 minutes**, then fill to the **100 ml** line and cap it tightly.
5. Fill out the lab's label and form, refrigerate the bottle, and keep it chilled on the way to the lab.

Elsewhere DOH says to test for **E. coli and coliform bacteria** "after you disinfect and flush your well." Until results show the water is safe, keep using bottled water (or another source your health department approves) for drinking, cooking and brushing teeth. DOH's flood page says that if you must use water you're unsure of, the **preferred method** is to boil it at a rolling boil for **one minute**. If you can't boil, add **1/8 teaspoon (8 drops) of unscented bleach per gallon** and let it stand 30 minutes. If it doesn't smell of chlorine by then, add another 8 drops and wait 15 more minutes.

**Then retest.** DOH: "You should consider retesting the well water after several weeks. If flooding and groundwater contamination is extensive, your well may be at risk of recontamination for some time."

### Which tests besides bacteria?

DOH's flood steps call only for the bacteria test. Its general advice is to test **after floods** and whenever the water's look, smell or taste changes. DOH's [Fact Sheet #3](https://health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/regulations/fact_sheets/fs3_water_quality.htm) ties tests to conditions. For example, test **volatile organic compounds** for an "odor of gasoline or fuel oil," and test **color, detergents, turbidity and total dissolved solids** for water that "appears cloudy, frothy, or colored." The fact sheet says all samples "should be analyzed by a laboratory certified by the NYSDOH Environmental Laboratory Approval Program (ELAP)."

### Keeping the next flood out

DOH suggests grading the ground away from the casing and installing a cap that keeps out water, insects and rodents. In flood-prone areas it recommends extending the casing "at least 1–2 feet above the highest recorded flood level." If the well floods often, think about drilling a new one somewhere it won't flood. DOH says a registered well driller should do the casing and cap work.

## In a drought: when the well runs low or dry

### First, make sure it's the drought

DEC's tips for well owners start with: "Determine whether your well is dry due to drought and not other issues. Sometimes the solution to a dry well is straightforward, like a broken valve or electrical issue. The only way to definitively tell if your well is dry is to measure the water level in the well. This task should be performed by a professional." DEC says to contact a [registered water well contractor](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/water/water-quantity/water-well-contractor-program) about your options.

### Don't pour trucked water into the well

DEC hosts an article by a NYS DOH engineer, "[If Your Well Goes Dry](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/water/water-quantity/drought/if-your-well-goes-dry)", which makes three points against having fire tankers fill a dry well:

- **Germs.** Tankers are often filled from ponds, streams, rivers and lakes, which may carry pathogens such as *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*. "Some pathogens are quite hardy and can survive in a tanker for weeks."
- **It drains away.** Residents "typically recover as little as 10 percent of the introduced water." Within a few hours, or a day or two at most, the rest is gone.
- **Certification.** "Few fire companies are properly certified to haul drinking water." State rules (State Sanitary Code Subpart 5-6) require bulk-water certification before a tanker carries drinking water.

The article recommends **certified haulers**, delivering into a clean, protected cistern or a food-grade supplemental storage tank (it says extra plumbing and possibly a suction pump will be needed). NYS DOH publishes the current **[NYS-Certified Bulk Water Facilities list](https://health.ny.gov/environmental/water/drinking/bulk_bottle/bulkwter.htm)** by county or district office. On 2026-10-30 it also listed two haulers whose certification ended in the past 12 months. Ask your local health department which certified hauler serves your area. The list doesn't say which ones deliver to homes.

### Longer-term fixes DEC lists

- Conserve water year-round.
- Add a water storage tank to get through dry periods.
- Clean or redevelop the existing well.
- Deepen it or drill a new well. If you drill a new one, "properly decommission the old well to protect groundwater quality."
- Public water hookup, where available. The DEC article calls it "a good bet" and DOH's private-wells page calls public water "the best option for household water."

### Report a dry well

DEC runs a **[Dry Water Well Reporting Form](https://arcg.is/0Tf8iy1)** for low or no water in a well. Anyone can use it: owners, drillers and environmental professionals. DEC says reported locations are generalized to the municipal and county level, and the form doesn't collect names, addresses or business details.

### Is there a drought where you are?

DEC sets drought stages for nine regions: **watch, warning, emergency, disaster**. On 2026-10-30 its [Current Drought Conditions](https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/water/water-quantity/drought/current-drought-conditions) page said: "Drought Region I is currently in Drought Watch." DEC's region map labels Region I as **Long Island**. The page prints no date for that status, and it can change at any time, so check the page itself. Under a watch there are "no statewide mandatory water use restrictions," though local water suppliers may require conservation. DEC adds: "If you are experiencing a water shortage, please contact your water supplier, local agricultural extension, or local health department."

### Testing after a drought

None of the NYS DOH or DEC pages we read gives a separate water-quality testing schedule for drought. What DOH does say applies: test for bacteria **after any well repair or maintenance** (deepening, pump work, a new tank) and after disinfecting. Test whenever your water's look, smell or taste changes. A **new well** should get the full set of initial tests in Fact Sheet #3 "prior to homeowner use", from arsenic and nitrate to hardness and sodium. That fact sheet also says some local health departments have their own testing requirements.

## Where to get the test done

Use a lab certified by NYS DOH's **ELAP** for potable water, or ask your local health department. Our [New York page](/labs/new-york/) explains the ELAP search and lists the Westchester County private-well labs. For routine testing outside an emergency, DOH says to test "at least once a year for bacteria and for other contaminants every 3-5 years." Its March 2024 release adds that "the best time to test private well water is in the late spring or early summer."

## What we could not verify

- **Costs.** No source here gives a price for disinfection, testing, hauled water or storage tanks. The DEC article quotes a tank price, but it's undated, so we left it out.
- **Current drought status outside Region I.** DEC's page named only Region I on 2026-10-30. We didn't check county or New York City determinations, which DEC says can differ.
- **Which certified bulk-water facilities deliver to homes.** The DOH list gives names, certificate numbers and addresses only.
- **The printed booklet is from February 2017** ("6562 2/17"). Its web version says the same things and shows no newer date. Some web addresses printed in the PDF (DEC's old well-contractor URL, Wadsworth's ELAP page) have since moved. The links on this page are the current ones.
