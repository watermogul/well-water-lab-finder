New Hampshire's bedrock is the problem. NHDES's drinking water page says that **"about half of the state's bedrock wells have radon at levels of concern, and an estimated 30% have arsenic at levels that exceed the 5 ppb limit that is enforceable in public water systems."** None of the three has a color, taste or smell, so the only way to know is a lab test. This page sums up what New Hampshire's own agencies say about each one. We read every source below on **2026-10-28**. Several NHDES fact sheets are a few years old (each one says so), and we note where that matters.

This is a summary, not medical or engineering advice. For your own results, use NHDES's free [Be Well Informed](https://www4.des.state.nh.us/DWITool/Welcome.aspx) tool or call the NHDES Drinking Water and Groundwater Bureau at (603) 271-2513.

## Is my well at risk? Drilled vs dug

- **Drilled (bedrock, also called artesian) wells** are where these three show up. NHDES fact sheet DWGB-3-11 says bedrock wells "can contain elevated concentrations of any these radionuclides, even if nearby bedrock wells have low concentrations."
- **Dug and point wells** (sand and gravel) are less likely to have them. NHDES says dug wells "are less likely to have problems with minerals (arsenic, radon, etc.) but are more likely to have issues with bacteria, low pH, road salt and nitrate." Its testing brochure says radon in water "may be omitted for wells that do not reach into bedrock (dug wells)."
- **Your neighbor's result does not tell you yours.** The arsenic fact sheet says that "due to our highly fractured bedrock, even wells within short distances can present very different water quality."

## Arsenic

**How common.** NHDES's arsenic fact sheet DWGB-3-2 (marked "accurate as of March 2021") says private wells in New Hampshire "have about a 25% probability of containing naturally occurring arsenic above 5 micrograms per liter (ug/L) or 5 parts per billion (ppb)." NHDES's newer bureau page puts it at an estimated 30% of bedrock wells. A March 2021 NHDES blog post estimated that **116,000 private-well users** were drinking water with more than 5 ppb.

**The limit.** EPA's enforceable limit (MCL) for public water systems is 0.010 mg/L (10 ppb). New Hampshire adopted a stricter **5 ppb** MCL for public water systems, effective **July 1, 2021**, and made 5 ppb an enforceable groundwater standard too. NHDES says New Hampshire was the second state after New Jersey to go below the federal level.

**Health effects named by NHDES.** Long-term drinking is linked to higher risk of cancer of the skin, bladder, lung, kidney, nasal passages, liver or prostate, plus diabetes, cardiovascular, immune and nervous-system effects. The fact sheet also cites New Hampshire research on birth outcomes, gestational diabetes and infant infections. It says skin absorption "is not a significant exposure path," so bathing is not a known risk.

**What to test.** Arsenic is part of NHDES's recommended list (and of the Standard Analysis at the state lab). If a result is **above 5 ppb**, NHDES recommends a **follow-up test for Arsenic-III** (arsenite) before buying a treatment system, because many systems remove Arsenic-III far less well. The 2021 fact sheet says the state Public Health Laboratory offered As-III testing, as do private labs. It printed a price that is now five years old, so check the current price with the lab.

**Treatment, in NHDES's words.** For most wells NHDES recommends **point-of-use** treatment (one kitchen tap for all drinking, cooking and ice) "as the most cost-effective and simplest solution." Options it describes: arsenic adsorption cartridges, reverse osmosis (with pre-oxidation if As-III is present), and, with strong caveats, certain filter pitchers. It recommends **whole-house** treatment at **100 ppb or higher** when people may drink from untreated taps, or when iron also needs removing. The fact sheet gives 2021 cost ranges for equipment. We don't repeat them here because they are dated. After installing treatment, NHDES recommends testing the treated water for arsenic **quarterly for the first year and twice a year after that**.

## Radon in well water

**How common.** NHDES's drinking water page: "About half of the state's bedrock wells have radon at levels of concern." Its radon fact sheet DWGB-3-12 (marked "accurate as of June 2019") says radon is "commonly found in bedrock and in water from bedrock (drilled) wells," while "dug wells and point wells tend to have minimal to no radon."

**Why water radon matters: it ends up in the air.** NHDES's rule of thumb: every **10,000 pCi/L** of radon in a home's water adds about **1 pCi/L** to indoor air. EPA, as NHDES reports it, considers breathing radon a much greater risk than drinking it. That's why NHDES says to **always test the air in your home as well**. EPA strongly recommends fixing indoor air at **4 pCi/L or more**.

**There is no legal limit for radon in drinking water.** NHDES's fact sheet says "there are no federal or state standards for radon in drinking water." Its own advisory levels for private wells:

- **10,000 pCi/L or more:** treat the water, together with indoor-air mitigation.
- **2,000 to 10,000 pCi/L:** water treatment "may be advisable" if indoor air is above 4 pCi/L.
- **Below 4,000 pCi/L:** retest air and water every three to five years.

**Testing tips from NHDES.** Radon in well water "can vary substantially from one test to another," so NHDES recommends **at least two radon tests, at least a month apart when possible,** before deciding on treatment. Because "a well that has high levels of radon is more likely to have high levels of uranium and/or gross alpha," it also recommends testing bedrock wells for **uranium and analytical gross alpha**.

**Treatment.** Radon has to be treated **whole-house**, since it escapes in the shower and elsewhere. NHDES's radionuclides fact sheet calls aeration the preferred technology. Granular activated carbon may be used at or below 10,000 pCi/L if the carbon is changed every 1–2 years, so radioactivity doesn't build up in the filter. Since January 1, 2015, radon-in-*air* mitigation designers and installers in New Hampshire must be nationally certified. NHDES says certification is *not* required for radon-in-water treatment.

## Uranium, gross alpha and radium

**How common.** NHDES's radionuclides fact sheet DWGB-3-11 (marked "accurate as of October 2019") cites a USGS study of bedrock aquifers in the northeastern US (Scientific Investigations Report 2011–5220). It found that **15%** of wells exceeded the uranium standard, **12%** the alpha-radiation standard and **3%** the combined-radium standard. That is a regional figure, not a New Hampshire–only one.

**The limits (EPA, for public water systems):** uranium **30 µg/L** (NHDES also gives it as 20 pCi/L); combined radium-226 + radium-228 **5 pCi/L**; "compliance" or net gross alpha **15 pCi/L** (analytical gross alpha minus the uranium activity). The health concern is mainly cancer. EPA's table also lists kidney toxicity for uranium.

**NHDES's three-step test plan:**

1. Collect samples for radon, uranium, gross alpha, radium-226 and radium-228. Ask the lab to **hold** the radium samples until the gross alpha result is in.
2. If gross alpha is **above 5 pCi/L**, have the lab run radium-226/228. Combined radium above 5 pCi/L means elevated radium.
3. If analytical gross alpha is **above 15 pCi/L**, subtract the uranium activity. If the net gross alpha is still above 15 pCi/L, NHDES says treatment is required.

**Treatment.** Uranium, radium and gross alpha are not volatile, so a **point-of-use** system (anion exchange cartridge or reverse osmosis for uranium) can work. NHDES warns that any whole-house **anion exchange** or mixed-bed resin **must be followed by a calcite neutralizer**, or "lead will be leached from the plumbing at potentially toxic levels." It also says not to send ion-exchange brine to a drywell, because that "will create a hotspot of radionuclides in your backyard." Send it to the septic system.

## What to order from the lab

NHDES's April 2026 testing guidance lists arsenic, radon and uranium among the tests every private-well user should run. It says to test for bacteria, nitrate and nitrite **every year** and the rest of the list **every 3–5 years**, and to test **before buying** a home with a well. NHDES also suggests more frequent testing after nearby rock blasting: before blasting begins, then several months to a year after.

## After the results come back

- Enter the numbers in NHDES's **[Be Well Informed](https://www4.des.state.nh.us/DWITool/Welcome.aspx)** tool. NHDES says it evaluates the Standard Analysis and suggests treatment if needed.
- Before you buy treatment, NHDES recommends an Arsenic-III test if arsenic is above 5 ppb, and at least two radon-in-water tests.
- Once treatment is in, test the **treated** water to confirm it works, and keep up with filter or media changes.
