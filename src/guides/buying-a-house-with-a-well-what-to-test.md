In most states nobody tests a private well for you when a house changes hands. Federal drinking-water rules don't cover private wells, and many states don't require a test at sale either. This page pulls together what state health and environmental agencies tell home buyers. We read every source below on **2026-11-03**.

**This is general guidance, not legal advice.** Contract terms (deadlines, who pays, what happens if a test fails) are set by your purchase agreement and your state's forms. Ask your agent or attorney.

## Is a water test required when you buy?

It depends on the state, and sometimes the town or the lender.

- **Oregon: yes, the seller must test.** The Oregon Health Authority says the Domestic Well Testing Act (ORS 448.271) "applies to the sale or exchange of real estate with a domestic well". The seller must test for **arsenic, nitrate and coliform bacteria** at an accredited lab. Results "are valid for 1 year." The seller has 90 days to send the results to the buyer, and to send the RET form and results to OHA. Spring wells, irrigation-only wells and wells on undeveloped land are exempt.
- **Virginia: not by the state.** VDH says its regulations "do not require well water quality testing for a property transfer," but "certain localities may require well water analysis" and lenders may require testing.
- **New Hampshire: not by the state.** NHDES still recommends that buyers test "before purchasing a home with a private well, if the seller has not already provided a recent water test."
- **New Jersey** has a Private Well Testing Act program for real estate sales. The NJDEP page blocked our fetch tonight, so we don't summarize its rules here; see our [New Jersey page](/labs/new-jersey/) for the official links.

For other states, start from our [state pages](/labs/) and ask the local health department.

## A lender's test may not be enough

VDH: "Many lenders require testing for bacteria, nitrate and lead at a minimum." It adds that, per EPA, these tests are "generally intended to ensure that the lender is not making a loan on a property with a faulty system... For this reason, lender required well tests may not be comprehensive evaluations of well water quality."

## What to test for

The lists differ. Here are three, side by side:

| Source | Tests named |
|---|---|
| **Oregon (required at sale)** | Arsenic, nitrate, coliform bacteria |
| **Typical lender minimum (per VDH)** | Bacteria, nitrate, lead |
| **NHDES (recommended for buyers and owners, April 2026)** | Arsenic; total coliform and *E. coli*; chloride; copper (stagnant and flushed); fluoride; hardness; iron; lead (stagnant and flushed); manganese; nitrate; nitrite; pH; radon; sodium; uranium; PFAS (PFHxS, PFNA, PFOA, PFOS) |

NHDES's list reflects New Hampshire geology (radon, arsenic, uranium are common there; see our [NH bedrock-well guide](/guides/new-hampshire-arsenic-radon-uranium-bedrock-wells/)). Elsewhere, EPA says to test at least for "total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH levels," and to "contact your local health department to find out what substances may be common in your area's groundwater." EPA's page also matches nearby conditions to extra tests, for example:

- Nearby intensive agriculture: nitrate, nitrite, pesticides, coliform bacteria.
- Lead plumbing or service lines: pH, lead, copper.
- Radon in indoor air or a radon-rich region: radon.
- Gas drilling nearby: chloride, sodium, barium, strontium.
- Coal or other mining nearby: metals, pH, corrosion.

More detail: [what to test well water for](/guides/what-to-test-well-water-for/).

## If the house has a treatment system

NHDES: "If the home has a water treatment system, the water test can be done after treatment to verify the quality of the treated water." That tells you what comes out of the tap today. It doesn't tell you what the treatment is removing, or whether it will keep working. Ask the seller what the system is for and for its service records.

## Questions to ask the seller

From NHDES's *Before You Buy a Property* sheet (SSB-6) and VDH's FAQ:

- **Where is the well, and what type is it?** NHDES notes wells "can be obscured by structures or even long grass."
- **Has the water been tested? Has the well ever been disinfected?** (NHDES.) A recent shock chlorination can affect a bacteria result, so ask when it was done. See [shock chlorination](/guides/shock-chlorination-well-disinfection/).
- **Is there a well record?** In New Hampshire, NHDES and the Water Well Board keep records of wells built since 1984 (NHDES OneStop). In Virginia, VDH says records weren't required before 1990, and the local health department can help find one. VDH also says "the current homeowner may have water testing and well maintenance records, and a well construction report."
- **Should the well itself be inspected?** NHDES encourages buyers "to have existing wells inspected to ensure they are properly sealed." VDH recommends that a stand-alone well inspection "be performed by a licensed water well service provider."
- **What about the septic system?** NHDES's sheet covers septic approvals and maintenance in the same checklist, since the two systems sit on the same lot.

## Timing

- Oregon says to get samples to the lab "within 24 hours of collection." Most results are back in "1-3 business days," but some tests, such as arsenic, "may take up to 10 business days or longer."
- Collect samples the way your lab says. Lead and copper "stagnant" samples need water that has sat in the pipes. See [how to collect a well water sample](/guides/how-to-collect-a-well-water-sample/).
- After you move in: NHDES says to test for bacteria, nitrate and nitrite every year, and the rest of its list every 3-5 years, "even if the water was tested when you purchased the home."
