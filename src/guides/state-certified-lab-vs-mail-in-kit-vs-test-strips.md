People use "test kit" for three different things. They are not equally reliable, and not all of them are accepted for the same purposes. This guide separates them.

## 1. A state-certified lab

You collect a sample in the lab's bottles (or the lab collects it), and an accredited lab runs standard methods. EPA's advice is to only use laboratories that are certified to do drinking-water testing. Nebraska's health department says lab testing is more accurate than paper strips. Use our [state lab pages](/labs/) or EPA's [directory of state programs](https://www.epa.gov/dwlabcert/contact-information-certification-programs-and-certified-laboratories-drinking-water) to find one.

**Good for:** a real baseline, a home purchase, anything with a health concern, or any case where someone else needs to rely on the result. **Watch for:** hold times. Bacteria samples have to reach the lab quickly. Penn State and the New Hampshire state lab both say within 30 hours, and neither takes bacteria samples at the end of the week (Penn State: not on Friday; NH PHL: not after noon on Friday or the day before a holiday).

## 2. A mail-in kit from an accredited lab

Some accredited labs send bottles and a prepaid or overnight return process to homeowners. Penn State's Agricultural Analytical Services Lab, which is accredited by Pennsylvania DEP, sells these through county extension offices. The sample is still analyzed by the accredited lab. You are choosing the collection method, not a different test.

**Good for:** people far from a lab drop-off point. **Watch for:** the mailing deadlines above. Some kits sold online by other companies are processed by a lab, but whether that lab is accredited for drinking water in your state is something to check yourself. We have not verified any commercial kit seller's accreditation, so we do not recommend or rank them.

## 3. Home test strips and color-change kits

You dip a strip or add a reagent and compare colors. They are cheap and immediate. The evidence we found is limited and shows a mixed picture:

- Southwest Nebraska Public Health Department hands out free nitrate/nitrite paper strips each May and September, as part of a partnership with the University of Nebraska-Lincoln's Citizen Science Water Quality Project. It calls strips a simple way to start monitoring, but says they are not as accurate as lab testing, and that if a strip shows a problem, or you want to be sure, you should send a sample to a certified lab.
- A 2025 study from UMass Amherst (published in the *Journal of Water and Health*) evaluated eight home test kits for iron, copper, manganese and fluoride. It found high variability between kits. Single-parameter kits were generally more consistently accurate than multi-parameter kits. None of the multi-parameter kits detected low levels of iron. Kits often over- or under-estimated concentrations, and instructions for interpreting results were inconsistent. The researchers said many kits perform fine in deionized water but not in real water, where background minerals or organics can interfere. The study covered only iron, copper, manganese and fluoride, so it says nothing about bacteria or nitrate strips.
- Study co-author Emily Kumpel advises interpreting kit results "with a healthy dose of skepticism" (the release says they "might be a good first cut on things, but it's not necessarily telling you all the information you need"), and says state environmental or public-health department websites list certified labs for anyone who wants something more reliable.

**Good for:** a quick screen, or watching for change between lab tests. **Not good for:** a purchase decision, a health decision, or proving a well is safe. A clean strip is not a clean bill of health.

## Which to pick

| Situation | What the sources point to |
|---|---|
| Buying a house with a well | Accredited lab, a full package (see [what to test](/guides/what-to-test-well-water-for/)) |
| Yearly check on a well with no history of problems | Accredited lab bacteria and nitrate test; some health departments give out free strips for a screen |
| Strip shows a problem | Confirm with an accredited lab (Nebraska health department's advice) |
| Staining or taste issues | A lab package covering iron, manganese, hardness, pH etc. (Penn State's aesthetics package is an example, see [costs](/guides/how-much-does-well-water-testing-cost/)) |
| Concern about health effects (infants, pregnancy, illness) | Accredited lab, and talk to your local health department |
