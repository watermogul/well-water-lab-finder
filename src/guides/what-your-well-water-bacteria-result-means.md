A well-water bacteria test almost always looks for **total coliform** bacteria, and usually also for ***E. coli***. This page explains what each result on the report means and what to do next. It uses the published guidance of Texas TCEQ (GI-433), Penn State Extension and Colorado State University (CSU) Extension, all read on **2026-11-04**. If your lab or local health department gives you different instructions, follow theirs.

## Why labs test for coliforms

Penn State calls coliforms "indicator organisms" because they "indicate the potential presence of disease-causing bacteria in water." Their presence "does not guarantee that drinking the water will cause an illness. Rather, their presence indicates that a contamination pathway exists" between a source of bacteria (surface water, septic system, animal waste) and the well. CSU explains that testing for every germ is "difficult and expensive", so labs measure coliforms instead. EPA notes that microorganisms in well water can cause "gastrointestinal illnesses and infections".

## The standard is zero

Penn State: "the drinking water standard requires that no coliform bacteria be present in drinking water. Fecal coliform and *E. coli* bacteria should also be totally absent." Any measurable count is above that standard. (Other limits: [reading your results / MCLs](/guides/reading-well-water-test-results-mcls/).)

## Result by result

| What the report says | What it means | What the sources say to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Total coliform absent** (not found, "no coliforms present", or "bacteriologically safe") | TCEQ: "This is good news." Your water is considered free of total coliform and *E. coli*. | Keep testing. Penn State recommends a total coliform test every year; CSU says every one to two years, and more often if the well is in poor condition, was flooded, or the septic system has failed. |
| **Total coliform present, *E. coli* absent** | TCEQ: "This is not good news." Penn State: a pathway exists for bacteria to reach the water. | TCEQ: don't use the water for drinking, cooking, making ice, washing dishes, bathing or cleaning. Boil it (full boil for two minutes), disinfect it, or use bottled water. Disinfect the well and retest. |
| ***E. coli* present** | Penn State: "much more serious than coliform bacteria alone because it indicates that human or animal waste is entering the water supply." | Same as above. Penn State adds: check the septic system, move or divert animal waste away from the well, and consider a sanitary well cap if yours is loose-fitting. |
| **A count, e.g. "32 per 100 mL"** or **"too numerous to count"** | CSU says results may be reported as absent, a count per 100 mL, or too numerous to count. Penn State says more coliforms means a greater chance that disease-causing bacteria are present. | CSU: if there is more than 1 total coliform per 100 mL or the report says "unsafe", disinfect the well and retest in one to two weeks. |
| **Unsuitable for analysis** | TCEQ: "a gray area." The lab could not reach a conclusion, perhaps due to a sampling error (rinsing the bottle, a cloudy or highly colored sample). | Read the directions again, ask the lab for guidance, and re-sample. TCEQ says you may also disinfect the well before retesting. (Sampling tips: [how to collect a sample](/guides/how-to-collect-a-well-water-sample/).) |

## After a positive result: the retest schedule

The sources agree: disinfect, then retest. They give different timing:

- **Penn State:** retest for total coliform "approximately 10 to 14 days" after disinfection. If clean, "wait an additional two to three months and have the water tested again." If bacteria come back in either test, "a continuous disinfection treatment system will be necessary."
- **CSU:** retest "in one to two weeks" (its shock chlorination steps say "after one week"). When retesting a well that tested positive, sample "as close to the well as possible." If bacteria persist, "the source of the contamination must be identified and eliminated."
- **TCEQ:** keep boiling or using other water until a lab reports "total coliform not found (absent)." If repeat tests still show coliforms, "consider adding continuous disinfection equipment."

How to disinfect: [shock chlorinating a private well](/guides/shock-chlorination-well-disinfection/).

## How often does disinfection alone fix it?

Penn State reports that in one of its studies, shock chlorination plus a sanitary well cap removed the bacteria for one year in about **15 percent** of contaminated wells. It worked best when counts were low ("fewer than 10 colonies per 100 mL") with no *E. coli*. CSU says replacing the well cap, casing and seal, or repairing the septic system, may be needed. If bacteria keep coming back, the cause is still there.

## Continuous disinfection, briefly

Penn State describes continuous chlorination (target about 0.3 to 0.5 mg/L residual chlorine and about 30 minutes of contact time) and ultraviolet (UV) light. It says UV units "are not recommended" where total coliform exceeds 1,000 per 100 mL or fecal coliform exceeds 100 per 100 mL, and that UV gives no residual disinfectant in the pipes. We don't list equipment or prices. Ask a licensed water treatment or well professional, and retest after installation.
